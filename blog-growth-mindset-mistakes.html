Growth Mindset & Classroom Culture

How Do Students With a Growth Mindset See Their Mistakes?

By Cara Zelas November 2026 7 min read

Students with a growth mindset see mistakes as information. Not as proof of failure, not as a sign they cannot do something -- but as data that tells them what to try differently next time.

That is a simple sentence. But getting a four-year-old -- or a seven-year-old -- to actually live that way is one of the more demanding things early childhood teachers do.

This post breaks down what a growth mindset relationship with mistakes actually looks like in young children, how it differs from a fixed mindset response, and what you can do in your classroom today to build it.

The short answer

Students with a growth mindset see their mistakes this way:

  • A mistake means I tried something that did not work yet -- not "I am bad at this."
  • A mistake is useful information -- it tells me something specific about what to do differently.
  • A mistake means trying again is worth it -- because effort leads to improvement.
  • A mistake does not mean anything permanent about who I am -- it is about this attempt, not about me.

That last point is the most important one for young children, who are still building their sense of self and are highly prone to confusing "I did something wrong" with "I am wrong."

"A growth mindset is not about being optimistic. It is about understanding that ability is not fixed -- that effort and strategy actually change what you can do."

Fixed mindset vs. growth mindset: what it looks like in real classrooms

The difference shows up in small moments. Here is what it sounds like in a preschool or kindergarten classroom when things go wrong:

Fixed mindset response

  • "I can't do it."
  • "This is too hard."
  • "I'm not good at this."
  • Pushes the work away
  • Crumples up the drawing
  • Refuses to try again
  • Gets upset when corrected
  • Avoids activities they might fail at

Growth mindset response

  • "I can't do it yet."
  • "This is hard -- let me try again."
  • "What could I do differently?"
  • Looks at the mistake and adjusts
  • Saves the drawing and tries again
  • Accepts feedback without shutting down
  • Watches others to learn a strategy
  • Chooses challenging tasks on purpose

Notice that growth mindset responses are not about being positive or confident. A child can say "this is hard" and still have a growth mindset -- the difference is in what comes next. Do they quit, or do they try again?

Five signals that a child has a growth mindset about mistakes

01

They stay at the task

When something does not work the first time, they pause and try again rather than giving up or escalating into frustration.

02

They ask for help without shame

They say "I need help" or "show me how" rather than hiding what they cannot do or refusing to try.

03

They accept feedback

When a teacher or peer points out an error, they take it in rather than getting defensive, shutting down, or crying.

04

They use specific language about effort

"I need more practice." "I am still learning that." "I did not get it right this time." These phrases signal they understand the relationship between effort and outcome.

05

They are curious about how others succeed

Instead of feeling threatened by a peer who can do something they cannot, they watch, ask, and try to learn the strategy.

06

They choose challenging tasks

Over time, children with a growth mindset actually seek out harder work -- because they have learned that difficulty is where growth happens, not where failure lives.

Why this is especially important for young children

Children ages three to eight are in a particularly sensitive period for building their relationship with challenge and failure. This is when core beliefs about ability are forming. A child who regularly hears "you are so smart" after getting things right is being set up, without anyone intending it, to treat mistakes as evidence that the smart label no longer applies. A child who hears "you worked hard on that" and "what could you try differently?" is building a framework that makes mistakes survivable -- and useful.

Carol Dweck's research, which introduced the terms "growth mindset" and "fixed mindset," found that children as young as four already show fixed or growth orientations -- and that these orientations are strongly shaped by the feedback and language they hear from adults.

This is not about telling children to be positive. It is about being precise in how you talk to them about effort, difficulty, and what a mistake actually means.

What teachers can do: six practical strategies

1

Change the language around mistakes

Instead of "good job" or "that is wrong," try: "that did not work -- what could we try next?" or "you found out one way that does not work -- that is actually useful." The goal is to make mistakes a source of information, not a verdict.

2

Share your own mistakes out loud

When you spell something wrong on the board, make a counting error, or misjudge how long something will take -- say so. "I made a mistake. Let me fix it." Young children need to see adults treat mistakes as normal and fixable.

3

Add "yet" to fixed-mindset statements

When a child says "I can't do it," respond with "you cannot do it yet -- but you are practicing." The word "yet" is small but it shifts the sentence from a verdict to a point in time on a continuing journey.

4

Use picture books that show characters persisting

Stories where characters try, fail, and try again -- and where the trying is what matters -- build a mental model for how to respond to difficulty. This is not abstract for young children: they store it as a script for what you do when something is hard.

5

Celebrate effort, not just outcome

"You kept going even when that was really hard" is more useful than "you got it right." Praising effort rather than results teaches children that the work they put in is what gets noticed -- and what leads somewhere.

6

Build a classroom culture where mistakes are shared

A morning meeting moment like "share one thing you are still learning" or "share a time something did not work this week" normalizes difficulty across the whole group. Children take their social cues from each other. When the whole class treats mistakes as part of the deal, individuals feel safer doing the same.

The goal is not for children to love making mistakes. It is for mistakes to feel survivable -- and informative. That is what makes the difference between a child who tries and a child who does not.

Common questions

How do students with a growth mindset see their mistakes?

Students with a growth mindset see mistakes as information rather than evidence of failure. They view mistakes as a natural part of learning, something to understand and learn from rather than hide or avoid. They ask "What can I do differently next time?" instead of "I am bad at this." In young children, this shows up as a willingness to try again, an ability to tolerate frustration without giving up, and an openness to feedback.

What is the difference between a growth mindset and a fixed mindset when it comes to mistakes?

A child with a fixed mindset sees mistakes as proof that they cannot do something -- evidence of a permanent limit. A child with a growth mindset sees the same mistake as feedback -- useful information about what to try differently. The key difference is not confidence or ability, but how the child interprets what a mistake means about them.

How do you teach a growth mindset about mistakes to young children?

The most effective approaches are: using specific language around effort and mistakes ("that did not work -- what could we try next?"), sharing your own mistakes as a teacher, adding "yet" to fixed-mindset statements, using picture books that show characters persisting, and praising effort rather than results. Programs like humanKIND Classroom that build these habits consistently across the year help children develop this orientation over time.

What does growth mindset look like in preschool and kindergarten?

In preschool and kindergarten, growth mindset shows up in small, concrete moments: a child trying again after the first attempt fails, saying "I need more practice" instead of "I can't do it", or watching a peer and asking "show me how" rather than giving up. At this age, growth mindset is not about abstract beliefs -- it is about whether a child has language and experience that tells them trying again is worth it.

Is growth mindset SEL?

Yes, growth mindset sits within social-emotional learning (SEL). It is part of the self-awareness and self-management domains of CASEL frameworks -- specifically the ability to recognize one's own emotions, have a realistic sense of self, and regulate responses to setbacks. In early childhood, growth mindset and social-emotional development are closely intertwined because both rely on a child's relationship with challenge, failure, and persistence.
