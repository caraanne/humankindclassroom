5 Picture Books That Teach Friendship Skills to Young Children

By Cara ZelasNovember 20268 min read

← Back to all articles

The right book at the right moment can open a conversation that a lesson never could. These five books have started some of the most meaningful discussions I have ever had with young children about friendship, belonging, and what it means to really see someone.

As a children's book author and early childhood educator, I spend a lot of time thinking about which stories do the deep work and which ones stay at the surface. The books on this list are the ones that go deep. They are for teachers who want their read-aloud time to do more than fill fifteen minutes before snack.

For each book I have included a note on how to use it and one discussion question that tends to open the room up.

"Stories let children try on other people's experiences before they have to live them. That is why a great book about friendship does more for social skills than a worksheet ever will."
Book 01

Each Kindness

by Jacqueline Woodson, illustrated by E.B. Lewis

A girl named Chloe repeatedly ignores a new classmate named Maya who tries to befriend her. Then one day Maya does not come back, and Chloe is left with the weight of all the kindness she did not show when she had the chance.

This book is for older Pre-K and Kindergarten through Grade 2. It is not a comfortable book, and that is exactly what makes it powerful. It teaches children that kindness is not just about doing nice things. It is about noticing when you have the chance and choosing to act.

Try asking: "Have you ever had a chance to be kind to someone and you did not do it? How did that feel afterward?"
Book 02

Enemy Pie

by Derek Munson, illustrated by Tara Calahan King

A boy has a perfect summer until a new kid moves in and becomes his enemy. His dad offers to make Enemy Pie to get rid of the enemy, but there is one condition: the boy has to spend a whole day with him first.

This is one of the best books for teaching children how friendships begin in unexpected places and how assumptions can keep us from people we might actually like. It is funny, warm, and always gets children talking.

Try asking: "Have you ever decided you did not like someone before you really knew them? What happened?"
Book 03

Last Stop on Market Street

by Matt de la Pena, illustrated by Christian Robinson

CJ rides the bus with his grandmother after church and asks why they do not have certain things other people have. His grandmother gently reframes everything through a lens of gratitude, beauty, and purpose.

This book is about friendship with the world: noticing people, connecting across difference, and finding beauty in ordinary things. It is a gorgeous entry point for conversations about empathy, community, and looking outward.

Try asking: "What is something beautiful that you have noticed lately that other people might walk past without seeing?"
Book 04

Big World of Little Dude

by Cara Zelas

Little Dude is a small dog exploring a very big world, navigating new experiences, new friends, and big feelings with warmth and humor. The series is designed specifically for young children who are working out how to show up for themselves and for others.

As the author, I wrote these books because I wanted stories that felt true to the messy, funny, tender experience of being small in a big world. Each book in the series is paired with discussion questions and can be used as a standalone read-aloud or as part of a broader social skills lesson.

Try asking: "Have you ever felt like Little Dude, like the world is very big and you are not sure where you fit? What helped?"
Book 05

Have You Filled a Bucket Today?

by Carol McCloud, illustrated by David Messing

Everyone carries an invisible bucket. Kind words and actions fill it. Unkind ones empty it. The book makes the abstract concept of emotional wellbeing concrete and tangible for young children in a way that sticks for years.

This is a perfect introductory book for the beginning of the year because it gives your class a shared language they can use all year. "You just filled my bucket!" is a phrase that changes classroom culture in the best possible way.

Try asking: "Who has filled your bucket recently? How did they do it? What can you do today to fill someone else's bucket?"

How to Get the Most Out of Any Book About Friendship

Whatever book you choose, three things make the difference between a story that lands and one that gets forgotten by recess:

  • Pause during the story, not just after. Ask "how do you think the character is feeling right now?" in the middle of the book, when the tension is real. That is when children are most engaged.
  • Connect it to real life. After the story, ask: "Has something like this ever happened in our class?" Not a hypothetical. A real question about their real lives.
  • Follow it with something active. An art activity, a role play, a game that practices the skill. The story plants the seed. The activity grows it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best picture books for teaching friendship in preschool?

Some of the most effective picture books for teaching friendship in preschool include Each Kindness by Jacqueline Woodson, Enemy Pie by Derek Munson, Last Stop on Market Street by Matt de la Pena, Have You Filled a Bucket Today? by Carol McCloud, and the Big World of Little Dude series by Cara Zelas.

How do you use picture books to teach social skills?

Pause during the story and ask open questions: "How do you think the character is feeling right now?" After the book, offer a brief activity or discussion that connects the story to real life in your classroom. The story plants the seed. The conversation and activity water it.

Can picture books replace a social skills curriculum?

Picture books are a powerful component of social skills teaching, but on their own they are not a complete curriculum. The most effective approach pairs rich stories with structured discussions, activities, and regular practice.

How often should I read books about friendship and kindness?

At least two to three times per week in Pre-K and Kindergarten. Social skills are built through repeated exposure, discussion, and practice. Reading and discussing books about friendship regularly gives children a growing vocabulary for their own feelings and relationships.

What questions should I ask after reading a friendship book?

Great discussion questions include: "How do you think the character was feeling?" "Has something like that ever happened to you?" "What do you think a good friend would do in that moment?" "Is there someone in our class who makes you feel the way this character felt at the end of the book?"
