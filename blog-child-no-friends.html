How to Help the Child Who Has No Friends in Your Class

By Cara ZelasNovember 20268 min read

← Back to all articles

There is one in almost every class. The child who is always a little bit outside the group. Who eats lunch alone. Who wanders during free play while others pair off easily. As a teacher, it is one of the hardest things to watch.

It is also one of the most important things you can address. Research consistently shows that peer relationships in early childhood have lasting effects on a child's confidence, learning, and wellbeing. The child who is socially isolated at five is at higher risk of struggling at ten, and at fifteen.

But helping this child is not as simple as telling other children to include them. Here is what actually works.

Step One: Observe Before You Intervene

Before you do anything, spend a week just watching. Try to understand what is actually happening. There is a difference between a child who is shy and needs a bridge, a child who lacks specific social skills, a child who is being actively excluded, and a child who is happy to play alone and does not actually need intervention.

Ask yourself: Does this child want friends but not know how to make them? Or are they genuinely content? Because the strategies are different, and the wrong approach can actually make things worse.

"The most important thing you can do for a child who is struggling socially is to make sure they have one real connection. Not the whole class. Just one person who is genuinely glad to see them."

Step Two: Identify the Barrier

Children struggle to make friends for different reasons. Some of the most common in Pre-K and Kindergarten:

  • Shyness or anxiety: The child wants connection but is too nervous to initiate.
  • Underdeveloped entry skills: They do not know how to join a game that is already in progress.
  • Impulsivity or aggression: They want to connect but push too hard and push children away.
  • Rigid play style: They want to play their way, every time, and others find this frustrating.
  • Social invisibility: They are quiet and overlooked, not disliked, just not noticed.

Each of these needs a slightly different response. Treating them the same is like giving every child with a stomachache the same medicine.

Practical Strategies That Actually Help

Pair, Do Not Group

Put the child with one specific peer for structured activities, not in a group of four where they can easily get lost. Choose the peer carefully: someone who is socially secure, patient, and shares at least one interest with the child. Set them both up for a task with a clear goal so there is natural conversation.

Teach Entry Skills Explicitly

Many children who struggle to make friends simply do not know how to enter play that is already happening. Practice this directly: "Watch what other children are doing. Find something you can do alongside them. Wait for a moment, then say: can I play too?" Role-play it. Practice it. The script matters for children who do not have it naturally.

Give Them a Social Role

Give the child a classroom job or role that naturally brings them into contact with others and gives them a moment of visibility. The child who leads the morning greeting, who chooses the book for read-aloud, who takes the attendance to the office. These are not big moments, but they are moments of presence and belonging that add up.

Narrate Positively to the Group

Without embarrassing the child, find genuine things to notice and name publicly: "I noticed Sam found a really clever way to build that tower this morning." This builds the child's social capital with peers without making it obvious that you are trying to help them.

Build the Whole Class Culture Around Inclusion

The most sustainable support for a child who struggles socially is a class culture where kindness and inclusion are genuinely valued. When the whole class is regularly practicing noticing each other, naming kind acts, and looking out for people on the edges, isolated children are less likely to stay isolated for long.

When to Involve Families and Specialists

If you have tried consistent, strategic support for a full term and the child is still significantly isolated or showing signs of real distress, it is time to have a conversation with the family and potentially refer to a school counselor or specialist. Early social struggles that go unaddressed do not tend to resolve themselves.

These conversations are not about alarming families. They are about bringing more people onto the child's team.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do you do when a child has no friends in class?

Start by observing rather than intervening immediately. Understand why the child is struggling. Is it shyness? Underdeveloped social skills? Then create low-stakes, structured opportunities for the child to connect with one peer at a time, rather than putting them in a group and hoping for the best.

How do you help a child who is excluded at school?

Talk to the child privately and warmly about what you have observed. Build a bridge between them and one other child with shared interests. Create structured activities that give the excluded child a natural role. And address the culture of the whole class, not just the individual.

Should a teacher intervene when a child has no friends?

Yes, but gently and strategically. Forced friendships do not work. The most effective interventions create natural conditions for friendship to develop: shared interests, shared tasks, shared experiences. Arrange the conditions and then step back.

When should a teacher be concerned about a child who has no friends?

It becomes a concern worth escalating when isolation is persistent across the whole school year, when the child shows signs of distress, when the child is being actively excluded, or when they seem to lack the basic skills to initiate or maintain a friendship.

How can a classroom program help a child who struggles socially?

A well-designed social skills program creates regular, structured opportunities for every child to practice social skills in a safe environment. Programs like humanKIND Classroom build a common language and culture around friendship, which makes it easier for all children, including those who struggle most, to find their place.
