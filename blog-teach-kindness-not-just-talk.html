How to Teach Kindness in the Classroom (Not Just Talk About It)

By Cara ZelasNovember 20267 min read

← Back to all articles

If telling children to be kind actually worked, every classroom would be full of kind children. The research is clear: kindness is not absorbed through instruction. It is built through practice.

Most early childhood educators already know this intuitively. We know that the child who pushes in line does not need a lecture about sharing. They need fifty small experiences of what it feels like to give, to receive, and to notice both.

Here are six approaches that actually move the needle, not just in the moment, but in the deep, habitual way that lasts.

Why "Be Kind" Does Not Work

Saying "be kind" or "be a good friend" gives children a destination with no map. Young children are concrete, literal thinkers. They need specific, observable actions. Not "be kind" but "you could pass the crayons to Mia." Not "be a good friend" but "go sit next to Leo because he looks like he needs a friend right now."

Specific is kind. Vague is not teaching.

"Kindness is not a character trait children either have or do not have. It is a set of skills that have to be practiced, just like reading and counting."

6 Ways to Teach Kindness Through Experience

01

Name Kind Acts Specifically When You See Them

Instead of "great job being kind," try: "I noticed you waited for Zara to finish her sentence before you spoke. That showed her you were listening. That was a kind thing to do." Specific narration teaches the whole class what kindness looks like in real life, without a single child feeling singled out or criticized.

02

Use Picture Books as Experience, Not Instruction

A well-chosen book puts children inside a story about kindness before you ask them to be kind. They feel what the character feels. They wonder what they would do. This kind of emotional experience builds empathy in a way that a lesson never can. After the story, ask: "Has something like that ever happened to you? What did you do?" Not "what should you do?" but "what did you do?"

03

Build In a Daily "Notice and Name" Moment

At the end of each day, ask the class: "Did you notice anything kind today? Who did it? What did they do?" At first children will name big acts. Over time they begin to notice smaller ones: someone held a door, someone picked up a dropped pencil, someone said sorry without being told. This trains the kindness-noticing muscle, which is the first step to being kind yourself.

04

Give Children Real Jobs That Help Others

Classroom helper roles are not just about efficiency. They are practice in caring for a community. The child who fills up the water bottles is learning to think of others. The child who helps a younger class is learning what it means to be a resource for someone smaller. Build these moments into your week intentionally, not just when you need a hand.

05

Model Kindness Toward Yourself Out Loud

Children watch how you treat yourself, not just how you treat them. When you make a mistake, say out loud: "Oh, I forgot my marker. That is okay. I will get another one." When you are tired, say: "I am feeling a little tired today, so I am going to take a deep breath before we start." This normalizes self-compassion and teaches children that kindness starts on the inside.

06

Make Kindness Physical and Visible

Abstract values need concrete anchors for young children. A kindness wall where children post or draw acts of kindness they observe. A kindness jar that fills with pom-poms when the class is kind. A heart on the board that grows bigger throughout the week. These physical representations make kindness tangible and give the class a shared language for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you teach kindness to preschoolers and kindergartners?

Kindness is taught through experience, not instruction. The most effective approaches include naming and celebrating kind acts when you observe them, creating structured opportunities for children to help each other, using picture books as a conversation starter, and modeling kind behavior yourself in real time.

At what age can children understand kindness?

Children as young as 18 months show empathic responses to others in distress, and by age 3 to 4, most children are developmentally ready to understand and practice basic acts of kindness. Pre-K and Kindergarten are ideal ages for intentional kindness teaching.

What is the best book for teaching kindness to young children?

Some of the most beloved books for teaching kindness include Each Kindness by Jacqueline Woodson, Have You Filled a Bucket Today? by Carol McCloud, Last Stop on Market Street by Matt de la Pena, and the Big World of Little Dude series. The best book is one that opens a genuine conversation with your class.

How do you make kindness a daily habit in the classroom?

Make kindness visible and specific. Name it when you see it. Create a physical reminder like a kindness wall or kindness jar. Build a daily moment into your routine where children notice and name kind acts. Avoid vague praise and be specific: "You noticed that Lena was sad and you sat with her. That was a kind thing to do."
