Small Group Social Skills Activities for Preschoolers

By Cara Zelas  |  November 2026  |  6 min read

Whole-class lessons are great for introducing ideas. But the real work of building social skills in young children happens in small groups. When you bring three to five children together for a focused activity, every child is actively involved, every turn is real, and you are close enough to notice, redirect, and name what you see as it unfolds.

These eight activities are designed specifically for small group time in preschool and Pre-K settings. Each one targets a specific skill, runs in 10 to 15 minutes, and requires minimal materials.

Why Small Group Works Better for Social Skills

In a class of fifteen children, a whole-group social skills lesson means that at any given moment, most children are watching rather than practising. In a small group of four, every child is in every interaction. The practice density is simply much higher.

Small groups also create a more manageable social environment for children who struggle. The audience is smaller, the dynamics are simpler, and the teacher can provide in-the-moment coaching that is impossible in a large group. For children with shyness, anxiety, or significant social skill gaps, small group is often the right starting point before moving to whole-class practice.

"In a small group, there is nowhere to hide and nowhere to hold back. Every child is part of every moment. That is exactly why it works."

8 Small Group Social Skills Activities

1

Pass the Compliment

Kindness + listening

Sit in a circle. One child starts by saying something kind about the person to their left: "I like how you always share the crayons." That child says thank you and gives a compliment to the next person. Go around the circle twice. Children who are stuck can pass, but always come back to them. The rule: the compliment must be specific to that person, not generic.

2

Build It Together

Turn-taking + negotiation

Give the group a single set of blocks with one rule: you can only add one piece at a time, and you have to ask "can I add now?" before each turn. No other rules. What emerges is a natural negotiation about what to build and how. The teacher observes and names what they see: "I noticed you waited even though you really wanted to add that piece."

3

Feelings Charades

Emotional literacy

Write feeling words on cards (happy, worried, proud, left out, surprised, embarrassed). One child draws a card and acts out the feeling without words. The group guesses. After each round, ask: "When do you feel this way?" This builds both the vocabulary of emotions and the ability to read them in others' faces and bodies.

4

Story Problem Cards

Problem-solving

Create simple illustrated cards showing common classroom problems: someone takes a toy, someone is left out of a game, two children want the same book. Hold up a card and ask the group: "What could they do?" Write down every suggestion without judging. Then ask: "Which idea would help everyone feel okay?" This teaches that there are multiple solutions and that some work better than others.

5

The Listening Game

Active listening

One child tells the group something: their favourite meal, their pet's name, what they did on the weekend. After they finish, the teacher asks the group: "What did you just hear?" Children recall details. The rule: when someone is speaking, everyone looks at their face. After the recall, the speaker says: "Yes, you were listening to me." This makes listening feel real and valued, not just polite.

6

Ask to Join Practice

Entry skills

Set up a small play scenario with two children already playing. The third child practices joining using the script: watch, find something interesting, then say "Can I play?" or "I like what you're making." After each attempt, give specific feedback: "I noticed you watched first before asking. That was smart." Practice being told no too, and what to do next.

7

Group Art with One Shared Piece

Sharing + collaboration

Give the group a single large piece of paper and a limited set of materials: one glue stick, four crayons, one pair of scissors to share. Their task: make something together. No other rules. The constraints force negotiation, turn-taking, and compromise to happen organically. Debrief afterwards: "What was hard? What felt good? What did you do when two people wanted the same crayon?"

8

What Would You Do? Puppets

Empathy + perspective

Use simple puppets or stuffed animals to act out a brief scenario: one puppet is sad because no one will play with them. Ask the group: "What do you think this puppet is feeling? What could the other puppets do?" Let children try out responses with the puppets themselves. Using puppets reduces the emotional load of difficult scenarios and makes it easier for children to explore different responses.

Getting the Most Out of Small Group Time

The teacher's role in these activities is not to run them but to notice and name. What you say matters more than the activity itself. "I saw you wait your turn even though it was hard. That was real patience." "I noticed you looked at Jonah's face when he was talking. That is what listening looks like." Specific acknowledgement in the moment is what turns a game into a learning experience.

Keep the groups consistent for at least a few weeks. When children know their small group partners, they take more risks, trust more, and practise more authentically. Random regrouping each session keeps things surface-level.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is small group time better for social skills than whole-class lessons?

In a whole-class lesson, most children are watching rather than practising. In a small group of three to five children, every child is actively involved in every interaction. They have more turns, more authentic practice moments, and more opportunity for the teacher to observe and redirect in real time.

How big should a small group be for preschool social skills activities?

Three to five children is ideal. With fewer than three, there is not enough social complexity for the skills to emerge naturally. With more than five, you lose the intimacy and high participation rate that makes small group work so effective.

Should I group children by social skill level for small group activities?

It depends on your goal. Mixed-skill grouping is often more effective for general social skills development, because children learn enormously from watching more socially skilled peers. Same-level grouping makes sense when you are targeting a specific skill with children who all need the same support.

How do I manage the rest of the class during small group social skills time?

Independent centres or free play rotations work well while you run a small group. Establish clear routines before you begin so the rest of the class knows exactly what to do. Choose activities that do not require significant teacher preparation so you can stay attentive to the whole room.

How often should I run small group social skills activities?

Two to three times per week is enough to see real skill development over time. If you can embed a brief social skills focus into existing small group rotations, you get practice without adding time to your schedule. Consistency over weeks is more important than frequency within a single week.
