How to Help a Child Who Is Being Bullied at School

By Cara Zelas  |  November 2026  |  7 min read

When a child tells you they are being bullied, or when you observe a pattern that looks like it, the feeling is immediate and clear: this needs to stop, and this child needs help. But what comes next is often less clear, especially in early childhood settings where the lines between developmental conflict and genuine bullying can blur.

This guide is for early childhood teachers who want to respond effectively, without making things worse for the child being hurt, without over-reacting to normal conflict, and without missing something serious when it is right in front of them.

First: Is It Bullying or Conflict?

This distinction matters because the response is different. Conflict is two children who both feel wronged and both have roughly equal power in the situation. Bullying involves a power imbalance and repetition: one child is consistently in the position of harm, and the other is consistently in the position of doing it. At the preschool age, most of what we see is conflict and developmental unkindness rather than true bullying. But patterns do develop, and when they do, they should be taken seriously.

Ask yourself: Has this happened more than once? Is it always the same child on the receiving end? Does the child doing the harm seem to know they have power over the other child? If the answer to those three questions is yes, it is worth treating as bullying rather than ordinary conflict.

"The child who tells you about being bullied has taken a significant risk. What you say in the next 30 seconds will determine whether they ever tell you something like this again."

What to Say When a Child Tells You

The first response is the most important. Thank the child. Say exactly this, or something close to it: "I am so glad you told me. That took courage. What is happening to you is not okay, and you did not deserve it. I am going to help."

Then listen. Do not minimise ("maybe they were just joking"), do not promise things you cannot guarantee ("I will make sure they never do it again"), and do not interrogate. Let the child tell you what happened at their own pace. Write it down afterwards so you have a record.

6 Steps to Take After a Child Reports Bullying

Step 1

Believe the Child and Document What You Heard

Write down the date, what the child said, and what they described. Even if you are not certain yet whether it is bullying, a written record is essential. If the situation escalates or involves parents or administration later, your documentation will matter enormously.

Step 2

Increase Your Observation

For the next several days, pay close attention to both children in unstructured times: free play, lunch, transitions. Watch for the behaviour the child described. Note what you see. Patterns become much clearer when you are actively looking for them rather than relying on memory.

Step 3

Speak Privately with the Child Doing the Harm

Address the behaviour directly, privately, and calmly. Name the specific behaviour: "I saw you tell Maya she could not join the game three times this week. That is unkind and it is not okay in our classroom." Give a clear expectation: "In our class, we include people." Do not shame, lecture, or label. Focus on the behaviour and what you expect going forward.

Step 4

Put Safe Structures Around the Child Being Bullied

This means managing the environment. Assign seating so the two children are not placed together. Ensure the child being bullied has a trusted peer nearby during unstructured time. Give them a classroom job or role that keeps them engaged and connected without having to navigate the other child.

Step 5

Talk to Both Families

Talk to the family of the child being bullied first. Be direct and factual about what you have observed and what steps you are taking. Then talk separately to the family of the child doing the harm. Frame this around specific behaviours, not character. Most families are willing to work with you when they feel respected rather than accused.

Step 6

Follow Up With Both Children Over Time

Check in with the child who was targeted every few days: "How are things going? Is anything bothering you?" A brief, private check-in signals that you are still watching and that they can still talk to you. Continue monitoring the behaviour of the child who was doing the harm. Document continued incidents and loop in administration if the behaviour does not change after your direct intervention.

Building a Classroom Where Bullying Has Less Room

The most powerful anti-bullying work happens before any incident occurs. Classrooms where children feel genuinely connected, where inclusion is a daily practice, and where every child knows they belong are classrooms where bullying is less likely to take hold. This does not mean it never happens. But the conditions that allow persistent bullying, such as social isolation, bystander passivity, and unclear norms around unkindness, can be directly addressed through intentional classroom culture building.

Morning meeting greetings, Compliment Circles, explicit teaching of bystander skills ("what do you do when you see someone being left out?"), and consistent modelling of how adults respond to unkindness all contribute to a culture where bullying behaviour runs up against strong resistance from the whole class, not just the teacher.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can preschoolers actually bully each other?

True bullying, defined as repeated intentional harm with a power imbalance, is rare in preschool. Most conflict in Pre-K and kindergarten is developmental. That said, patterns of one child consistently targeting another do appear in early childhood settings and should be taken seriously even if the child doing the targeting does not fully understand what they are doing.

What are the signs that a child is being bullied at school?

Signs include not wanting to come to school, unexplained changes in mood or appetite, frequent complaints of stomach aches or headaches on school days, avoiding certain children or areas of the playground, and reports from the child that other children are unkind to them. Younger children often just say "they are mean to me" or "they never let me play."

What should I say to a child who tells me they are being bullied?

Thank them for telling you. Say: "I am really glad you told me. That is a brave thing to do. What happened is not okay, and you did not deserve it. I am going to help." Then listen without minimising or catastrophising.

Should I confront the child who is doing the bullying?

Yes, but thoughtfully. Address the behaviour directly with the child, privately and calmly. Name the specific behaviour and its impact. Avoid shaming the child or labelling them as a bully. Many children who bully others have their own unmet needs or social skill deficits. The goal is behaviour change, not punishment.

When should I involve parents in a bullying situation?

Involve both sets of parents fairly early. Talk to the family of the child being bullied first. Be factual about what you have observed and what you are doing about it. Talk to the family of the child doing the bullying separately and frame the conversation around specific behaviour rather than character. Keep records throughout.
