How to Help a Child Who Struggles with Transitions at School

By Cara Zelas  |  November 2026  |  6 min read

You know the moment. Clean-up time is announced, and one child dissolves. The class is moving to the mat, and one child is frozen at the table. It is time to go outside, and one child is on the floor refusing to put on their shoes.

Transitions are some of the most challenging moments in the preschool day, not just for the children who struggle with them but for the teachers trying to move fifteen other children forward at the same time. Here is what is actually happening, and what you can do about it.

Why Transitions Are Hard for Young Children

A transition requires a child to do several things at once: stop what they are engaged in, hold the new expectation in working memory, shift their body and attention to a new context, and regulate the feelings that come with any of that. For adults this is automatic. For a three or four year old whose prefrontal cortex is still developing, it is genuinely hard work.

Some children find transitions harder than others because of temperament, anxiety, sensory sensitivities, or simply because they are deeply engaged in something and their brain has not had enough warning to shift gears. The good news is that transition skills can be taught. They improve with practice, structure, and the right kind of support.

"A child who melts down at clean-up time is not being defiant. They are showing you that their brain needs more preparation to handle change."

7 Strategies That Actually Help

Strategy 1

Give a Two-Part Warning

Warn twice: five minutes before, then one minute before. The first warning lets the brain begin preparing. The second warning activates follow-through. Children who receive consistent warnings before transitions adapt to them surprisingly quickly. Within a few weeks, many will start initiating the transition themselves when they hear the first warning.

Strategy 2

Use the Same Signal Every Time

A consistent auditory or visual signal removes ambiguity. A specific song, a chime, a clap pattern, or a visual timer all work. The signal should mean only one thing: it is time to transition. When children know exactly what the signal sounds and looks like, their body starts responding before their brain has even processed it consciously.

Strategy 3

Give the Child a Role in the Transition

Children who struggle with transitions often do better when they have a job to do during the change. "You are the Light Switcher for clean-up time." "You are the Line Leader today." Having a role gives the child a reason to move and something to focus on other than the loss of what they were doing.

Strategy 4

Name What Is Coming Next, Not Just What Is Ending

Many transition warnings focus on what is stopping: "Clean-up time now." A more effective warning names both sides: "In five minutes, we are going to finish our painting and then it will be story time on the mat." Knowing what comes next makes leaving the current activity less threatening.

Strategy 5

Offer a Bridge Object for High-Anxiety Children

For children with significant transition anxiety, a bridge object can help. This is a small, calming item they carry from one activity to the next. A smooth stone, a small toy, or a stuffed animal can give the nervous system something familiar to hold onto while everything else changes. Fade the object out gradually once trust is established.

Strategy 6

Acknowledge the Feeling Before Directing the Behaviour

If a child is already distressed at a transition, trying to redirect or instruct them before acknowledging what they feel will escalate rather than calm. Try: "I can see you are really not ready to stop. That is hard. We are going to the mat now, and I will sit next to you." Validation first, then the expectation, then the support.

Strategy 7

Debrief and Celebrate Progress

When a child who normally struggles with transitions handles one well, name it specifically and immediately. "You put your things away when I called clean-up. That was really mature." Children who struggle with transitions often receive more attention for their difficulties than for their successes. Flipping that ratio changes the trajectory.

What About the Child Who Truly Cannot Cope?

If a child's transition struggles are severe, frequent, and unresponsive to these strategies after several consistent weeks, it is worth considering whether there is an underlying factor at play. Anxiety disorders, sensory processing differences, and developmental differences can all contribute to significant transition difficulties. Talk to the child's family, loop in your school's support team if one exists, and document what you are observing.

Transitions are a window into a child's self-regulation capacity. What you do in those moments matters. The more you can make transitions predictable, warm, and skill-building, the more you are helping children develop the capacity they will rely on for the rest of their lives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do some children struggle so much with transitions at school?

Transitions require the brain to stop one activity, hold the new expectation in working memory, and shift to a new context all at once. For children with lower self-regulation, anxiety, or difficulty with the unexpected, this cognitive and emotional load can be overwhelming. It is not defiance. It is a brain that has not yet developed the tools to manage change smoothly.

What is the best transition warning for preschoolers?

The two most effective transition warnings are a time warning and a visual cue. A time warning sounds like: "In five minutes we are going to clean up and move to the mat." A visual cue might be a timer, a song, or a specific sound. The key is consistency.

Should I let a child skip a transition if they are struggling?

Occasionally allowing extra time or a gentler pathway is supportive, not permissive. What you want to avoid is making transitions optional in a way that teaches the child that distress is a way to stay in control. Offer support and scaffolding, not exemption.

How can I prepare a child for a big transition like a field trip or fire drill?

For big or unusual transitions, preview and prepare in advance. Tell the child what will happen, in what order, and what it will look and feel like. Use pictures if possible. Walk through the sequence the day before. Children who know what is coming are far better equipped to manage the experience when it arrives.

When should I involve parents about a child's transition struggles?

Involve parents early when a child's transition difficulties are significantly affecting their ability to participate in classroom life, when the distress is intense and frequent, or when you notice the same child struggling across multiple contexts. Parents often have strategies that work at home, and consistency between home and school makes a real difference.
