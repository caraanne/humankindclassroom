What to Say When Two Kids Won't Stop Fighting Over a Toy

By Cara ZelasNovember 20266 min read

← Back to all articles

Conflict over toys is not a problem to eliminate. It is one of the richest social learning opportunities in your entire school day. The key is knowing what to do with it.

In Pre-K and Kindergarten, fights over toys happen constantly. They feel exhausting, especially the fifth time before lunch. But every single one of them is practice in the skills your students will use to navigate relationships for the rest of their lives: perspective-taking, communication, negotiation, and repair.

Here is a step-by-step approach and a real script you can use, starting today.

Why "Just Share" Does Not Work

The instinctive teacher response is to say "share the toy" or "take turns" and move on. The problem is that this solves the conflict for you, not for them. It teaches children that an authority figure will come and divide things, so there is no reason to develop their own conflict skills.

Taking the toy away entirely is even less useful. It removes the problem without building anything in its place.

The approach that actually builds skills takes three to five minutes longer in the moment and saves hours over the course of the year.

"Every conflict is a free lesson in problem-solving. The toy fight is not the interruption. It is the curriculum."

The Script: What to Say, Step by Step

Step 1: Get Down and Get Calm

"I need everyone to stop for a second. I can see something is happening here. Let's all take a breath."

Get down to both children's level. Use a calm, low voice. Your calm body language is doing half the work before you say anything.

Step 2: Hear Both Sides Without Judgment

"Marcus, can you tell me what happened? [Listen.] And Maya, can you tell me what happened? [Listen.] Thank you both for telling me."

Do not decide who is right. Do not interrupt. Even if the story is factually wrong, what you are doing here is making sure each child feels heard. That is what allows them to hear each other.

Step 3: Name the Problem

"So here is what I understand. You both want the red truck at the same time. You have a problem. The problem is that there is one truck and two people who want it right now."

Naming the problem neutrally takes the blame off both children and frames it as something to solve together, not something someone did wrong.

Step 4: Invite Solutions

"Can you think of a way to solve this problem that is fair to both of you? What are some ideas?"

Give them real time to think. If they are stuck, offer two or three options: taking turns with a timer, playing together with the truck, or choosing a different toy for now. Let them choose rather than telling them which option to take.

Step 5: Acknowledge the Resolution

"That was really hard, and you figured it out together. That is what problem solvers do."

Name the skill they just used. Explicitly. Children do not automatically know when they have done something important. You have to tell them.

What to Do When It Does Not Work

Sometimes children are too activated to problem-solve. If that is the case, separate them first, give them both a minute to cool down, and then come back to the conversation. Do not try to reason with a child who is in the middle of big feelings. Wait until they are calm enough to hear you.

If the same two children are in conflict every day, that is worth a separate conversation with each of them and potentially their families.

Building These Skills Proactively

The best time to teach conflict resolution is before the conflict happens. Use circle time, role play, and stories to practice the script before children need it in a real moment. When they have heard the words before, they are far more likely to remember them under pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you handle conflict over toys in a preschool classroom?

Get down to both children's level. Make sure both feel heard before you offer any solution. Name what each child is feeling. Then invite them to solve the problem together: "You both want the red truck. That is a problem. Let's think of a way to solve it that is fair to both of you." Give them real choices and let them decide.

Should a teacher take a toy away when two children are fighting over it?

Taking the toy away ends the conflict but misses the learning opportunity. Unless the children are becoming physical, the most valuable response is to stay with both children, slow things down, and guide them through solving the problem themselves. Each conflict is practice for the next one.

What do you say to young children who are fighting over a toy?

Start by acknowledging both children: "I can see you both really want that toy." Then give each child a chance to tell their side without interruption. After both have spoken, say: "You both have a problem. Let's think of a way to solve it." Then offer two or three concrete options and let them choose.

How do you teach sharing to preschoolers?

True sharing is a complex skill most children are not developmentally ready for until age 3 or 4. The most effective approach is to give children real language for it, model it yourself, and narrate and celebrate it whenever you see it happening naturally.

What is the best conflict resolution approach for early childhood?

The most effective approach follows four steps: calm both children; give each a chance to tell their side while the other listens; name the problem clearly and neutrally; invite both children to generate solutions and choose one together. The goal is not to fix this specific fight but to teach a process children can eventually use on their own.

Teach the skills before the next conflict happens.
